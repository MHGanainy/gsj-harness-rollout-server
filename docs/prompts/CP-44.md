# CP-44 — the examples, in brief

Scope: the examples repo's front door, for someone deciding whether to
train with this.

**Audience**: a trainer engineer. They have a model, a trainer, and GPUs.
They want to know what the bridge gives them and what it costs.

**Freeze-lift: the examples repo. Plus `docs/**` in the library.**

Read first: the examples `README.md`, both bridge READMEs, `FINDINGS.md`,
and `docs/reports/CP-16.md` / `CP-20.md` (what each bridge is and isn't).

## Step 1 — What you get, in one paragraph and one picture
Traces in, batches out, for two trainers. The picture: the rollout
server's trace → the bridge → your trainer's batch type, with the three
assertions visible on the path. They are the reason the bridge exists.

## Step 2 — The two bridges, compared
A short table: which trainer, what batch shape, what's proven (CP-17's
loop, CP-21's loop), what each still needs from you (reward, weight sync,
grouping). **Both are consumer code, not library code** — say so, becaus who owns a bug.

## Step 3 — What it costs, measured
The numbers a trainer needs before committing GPU time: collection rate,
the qualification standard, the reward-density reality, the replay floor,
the sync mechanism and its downtime. All from CP-17, CP-21, CP-32.

**Do not make it sound easy.** A trainer who discovers the 19-attempt
qualification rate on their own cluster is worse off than one who read it.

## Step 4 — What is not solved
CP-17's and CP-21's "what a real training run would need" lists —
non-restart weight sync, P3 under concurrency, denser reward, the F-08
guard, throughput, checkpoint retention. These are the trainer's, and
naming them is what makes the rest credible.
